Beetlebot

A group project for ME 2505: designing and building a 3 lb combat robot for the course's yearly competition, where it battles other teams' robots. My team of three is building a two-wheel, zero-turn prototype, and I own the CAD and hardware design.

Course ME 2505, Villanova University Team 3 students Role CAD and hardware Timeline Aug. 2026 – Present
Combat Robot Prototype

Overview

Beetlebot is a semester-long project for ME 2505 that runs in the background alongside the course's lab work and builds toward its yearly combat robot competition. We're building toward a working prototype: the interior electronics are soldered together with the motors and batteries, the wheels are 3D-printed, and we are now testing an updated 0.05 in aluminum shell.

The arena has lawn mower blades in all four corners, so we chose the no-weapon track and plan to use those blades to our advantage: zip around the arena, avoid our opponent's attacks, and force them into the blades.

That plan sets our priorities: endurance, durability, serviceability, and a simple design that leaves time to practice driving and controlling the robot, which is a challenge in itself. The goal is a lightweight, maneuverable beetlebot with a zero turn radius and maximum power and torque through the wheels, all within a 3 lb limit on the full assembly.

Electronics

My teammates handle the electronics and controls; I own the CAD and hardware. The team has soldered the drive motors, batteries, and interior electronic components together into one working power and drive unit.

Wheels & Ground Clearance

The two-wheel, zero-turn drivetrain sets the wheel design. I designed the 3D-printed wheels around the motor placement inside the shell, and modeled a ball-joint offset in SolidWorks to target a 7 mm minimum ground clearance, within the margin of error of manufacturing accuracy.

Mass Distribution

Added mass is concentrated near the robot’s center of gravity to bring the full assembly up to the 3 lb limit. Keeping that mass low and central improves stability in collisions, so opponents can’t push the robot around as easily, and minimizes its yaw moment of inertia for maximum turning speed.

Aluminum Shell

We are testing an updated 0.05 in aluminum shell: sheet stock cut into panels on a stomp shear, with bolted angle brackets joining adjoining faces. The new version is taller with a longer sloped front wedge, and its floor panel now has cutouts for the drivetrain. Testing focuses on how the shell and the added mass near the center of gravity work together to hit the 3 lb limit while keeping the robot stable under hits and quick to turn.

Earlier Shell Iteration

The first shell design, kept here to show the design process: a lower profile with a shorter front wedge, built from the same 0.05 in aluminum sheet and bolted angle brackets.

Structure & Serviceability

The layout is designed around structural integrity and serviceability: the robot has to hold up to hits and still be quick to open and repair between matches.

The motor structure is fastened in as well, keeping the drivetrain secure under impact while staying easy to reach and service.
