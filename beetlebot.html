Beetlebot

A group project for ME 2505: designing and building a 3 lb combat robot for the course's yearly competition, where it battles other teams' robots. My team of three is developing a working prototype, and I own the CAD and hardware design.

Course ME 2505, Villanova University Team 3 students Role CAD and hardware Timeline Aug. 2026 – Present
Combat Robot Prototype

Overview

Beetlebot is a semester-long project for ME 2505 that runs in the background alongside the course's lab work and builds toward its yearly combat robot competition. We're still in the early stages of development: the interior electronics are soldered together with the motors and batteries, the wheels are 3D-printed, and the sheet-metal shell design and panel cutting plan are finalized. Our first drive test is still ahead.

The arena has lawn mower blades in all four corners, so we chose the no-weapon track and plan to use those blades to our advantage: zip around the arena, avoid our opponent's attacks, and force them into the blades.

That plan sets our priorities: endurance, durability, serviceability, and a simple design that leaves time to practice driving and controlling the robot, which is a challenge in itself. The goal is a lightweight, maneuverable beetlebot with a zero turn radius and maximum power and torque through the wheels, all within a 3 lb limit on the full assembly.

Electronics

My teammates handle the electronics and controls; I own the CAD and hardware. The team has soldered the drive motors, batteries, and interior electronic components together into one working power and drive unit.

Wheels & Ground Clearance

The two-wheel, zero-turn drivetrain sets the wheel design. I designed the 3D-printed wheels around the motor placement inside the shell, and modeled a ball-joint offset in SolidWorks to target a 7 mm minimum ground clearance, within the margin of error of manufacturing accuracy.

Mass Distribution

The layout keeps mass low and near the center of the robot to lower its center of gravity and its yaw moment of inertia, which helps it turn quickly and resist flipping. Added mass is planned up to the 3 lb limit so opponents can't push the robot around as easily.

Aluminum Shell

The shell design and panel cutting plan are finalized: 0.05 in aluminum sheet stock cut into panels on a stomp shear, with bolted angle brackets joining adjoining faces.

Structure & Serviceability

The layout is designed around structural integrity and serviceability: the robot has to hold up to hits and still be quick to open and repair between matches.

The motor structure is fastened in as well, keeping the drivetrain secure under impact while staying easy to reach and service.
